The Parents' Evening A classroom, after hours
Role A
The teacher

A bright student's grades have dropped sharply. You suspect it is phones and late nights, but you need the parent on your side, not defensive.

Role B
The parent

You think your child is doing fine and that the school piles on too much pressure. Hear the teacher out, but stand up for your child.

The teacher invites the parent to sit down.
The Phone Ban Head teacher's office
Role A
The head teacher

You are bringing in a total phone ban from Monday. You believe it will transform focus. Explain it firmly, but you would like the students onside.

Role B
The student representative

You think a blanket ban is unfair and unworkable, and that phones have real uses. Argue for a compromise without being dismissed as a troublemaker.

The head teacher slides the new policy across the desk.
The Star Pupil A quick word after class
Role A
The pushy parent

You want your child moved up into the top set immediately. You are sure they are being held back. Press your case, but you do need this teacher's goodwill.

Role B
The teacher

The child is good, but not quite ready, and the top set is already stretched. Be honest about that without crushing the parent's pride in their child.

The parent is waiting by the door as the class files out.
The Budget A governors' meeting
Role A
The head teacher

The budget is cut and something has to go: either the art and music programme, or a teaching assistant who supports struggling children. Argue for your choice.

Role B
The school governor

You want to protect exam results above all, because that is what the school is judged on. Push back on anything that risks the league-table position.

The head lays out the two options on the table.
The League Table The school gates, camera rolling
Role A
The journalist

The school has just dropped sharply in the national rankings. You want a clear, quotable explanation. Be persistent, but you are not there to ambush anyone.

Role B
The head teacher

You believe the table is misleading and hides real progress with disadvantaged pupils. Defend your school without sounding as if you are making excuses.

The journalist holds out the microphone.
The Private Tutor A comfortable living room
Role A
The wealthy parent

You want to hire this tutor for intensive one-to-one sessions to get your child into the very best school. Money is no object. Close the deal today.

Role B
The tutor

You need the work, but you are uneasy that private tutoring tilts the whole system against poorer children. Decide whether, and how, to take the job.

The parent names a generous hourly rate.
The Career Choice Around the kitchen table
Role A
The teenager

You want to skip university and train as an electrician. You are certain it is the right move for you. Convince your parent it is a serious plan, not a cop-out.

Role B
The parent

You always assumed your child would go to university, and you worry they will regret this. Voice your fears without simply forbidding it.

The teenager brings it up over dinner.
The New Method The staff room
Role A
The new teacher

You want to ditch the textbook and teach through projects and discussion. You are excited and sure it works. Win over a respected colleague you will need on your side.

Role B
The veteran teacher

You have seen fads come and go, and you worry the students will not be ready for the exam. Challenge the idea, but stay open if the argument is good.

The new teacher pitches the idea over coffee.
The Homework A quiet corner of the library
Role A
The student

Role B
The teacher

You are almost certain, but you have no proof. You want the student to be honest, not so you can punish them, but so you can actually help.

The Inspection The back of the classroom
Role A
The inspector

You have watched one lesson and formed concerns about the pace and challenge. You must feed this back honestly, knowing your report carries real weight.

Role B
The teacher

That lesson was not your best, and this class has particular needs the inspector did not see. Explain the context without sounding defensive or making excuses.

The inspector closes their notebook and turns to you.

Reflective journal

Think about

  • The one teacher or subject that has stayed with you the most
  • One thing your schooling did well for you, and one thing it failed to do
  • Whether you were "good at school," and what that phrase even means
  • A moment when you felt reduced to a grade or a test result
  • What you would most want to be different for a child of your own
