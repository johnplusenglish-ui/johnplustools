The shape of the work

Each assignment is really read around a topic, gather sources, plan, write, reflect, submit. The module is built so the writing skills from Assignment 1 feed into 2 and 3, and you are expected to act on the feedback from each one.

    Learning outcomes, the full assessment breakdown, and the rules on communication and extensions.

Learn the content · Part 1

Developmental Psychology

the theorieskey studiescriticisms
What this covers

Developmental psychology studies how and why people change across the lifespan — physically, cognitively, socially and emotionally. Below are the core approaches you need to understand in their own right, each with the evidence behind it and where it is weak. The marks come from explaining and evaluating a theory, so always learn the criticisms, not just the claims.

First, the big debates

Every developmental theory takes a position on these. Naming the debate a theory sits in is an easy way to evaluate it.

DebateThe questionWho sits where
Nature vs nurtureIs development driven by biology/genes, or environment/experience?Nature: Chomsky, Bowlby, Piaget (maturation). Nurture: Skinner, Bandura, Vygotsky (culture).
Stages vs continuityDoes development jump through distinct stages, or build up gradually?Stages: Piaget, Erikson, Freud. Continuous: learning theorists.
Critical vs sensitive periodsMust some things be learned in a fixed window, or just more easily?Critical: Bowlby, Chomsky (strong form). Sensitive: most modern research.

The approaches

Piaget — Cognitive development

cognitive · stages · nature

Children are little scientists who actively build understanding by interacting with the world.

schemaassimilationaccommodationequilibrationobject permanence
Core idea

Thinking develops through four universal, fixed-order stages. Children build mental structures called schemas; they fit new information into existing schemas (assimilation) or change the schema to fit (accommodation), driven to resolve the discomfort of not understanding (equilibration).

  • Sensorimotor (0–2): learning through senses and action; the big achievement is object permanence (things still exist when out of sight).
  • Preoperational (2–7): symbols and language, but egocentric and lacking conservation.
  • Concrete operational (7–11): logical about concrete things; grasps conservation.
  • Formal operational (11+): abstract and hypothetical reasoning.
Key study / evidence

Piaget's object-permanence tasks (hiding a toy under a cloth) and conservation tasks (same water, taller glass) show predictable errors at predictable ages. The "three mountains" task demonstrated egocentrism in preoperational children.

Criticisms

He underestimated children — with simpler methods they show object permanence and reduced egocentrism earlier (e.g. Baillargeon's violation-of-expectation studies). Stages are less tidy and universal than claimed, cognitive development is more continuous, and he downplayed the role of culture and social interaction (Vygotsky's central objection).

A1The sensorimotor and early preoperational stages map directly onto toddlers — object exploration and the beginnings of pretend play are easy behaviours to anchor to Piaget.
Sprouts · Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development

Vygotsky — Sociocultural theory

cognitive · nurture · social

Cognition is built through social interaction and culture — we learn with and from more knowledgeable others.

ZPDscaffoldingmore knowledgeable otherprivate speech
Core idea

Development is driven by social interaction, not solo discovery. The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) is the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help. A more knowledgeable other supports the child through this zone with scaffolding (help that is gradually withdrawn). Language is the key cultural tool; private speech (talking to oneself) becomes internal thought.

Key study / evidence

Wood and Middleton (1975) showed mothers who adjusted their help to the child's level (true scaffolding) produced better problem-solving. Tutoring and peer-collaboration studies consistently show gains within the ZPD.

Criticisms

Vague and hard to measure — "ZPD" and "scaffolding" resist precise testing. It overplays the social and underplays biology and individual differences, and says little about emotional or motivational development. Applies less well to cultures where guided learning works differently.

A1Any moment where an adult guides, prompts or demonstrates for the child is a scaffolding/ZPD example — a strong contrast to Piaget's lone learner.
Sprouts · Vygotsky's Theory of Cognitive Development

Bowlby & Ainsworth — Attachment

social/emotional · nature

Infants are biologically driven to form a close bond with a caregiver, and that bond shapes later relationships.

monotropyinternal working modelsecure baseStrange Situation
Core idea

Bowlby: attachment is an evolved survival mechanism. Infants form one primary bond (monotropy) during a critical period, use the caregiver as a secure base for exploration, and build an internal working model — a template for future relationships. Ainsworth measured attachment with the Strange Situation (observing separations and reunions), identifying types: secure, insecure-avoidant, and insecure-resistant (later, disorganised).

Key study / evidence

Bowlby's "44 Thieves" linked early maternal separation to later emotional problems. Ainsworth's Strange Situation found most infants secure, and caregiver sensitivity predicted security. Harlow's monkeys showed comfort beats food in forming attachment.

Criticisms

Monotropy is overstated — infants form multiple attachments. The Strange Situation may measure the specific relationship, not a fixed trait, and is culturally biased (van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg found cross-cultural variation). The 44 Thieves study was correlational and retrospective.

A1Proximity-seeking, checking back to the caregiver, and distress on separation are textbook attachment behaviours in a toddler clip.
Sprouts · The Attachment Theory: How Childhood Affects Life

Erikson — Psychosocial development

social/emotional · stages

We develop across the whole lifespan by resolving a social "crisis" at each of eight stages.

psychosocial crisistrust vs mistrustautonomy vs shame/doubt
Core idea

Eight stages, each a crisis between two outcomes; healthy development means resolving each in favour of the positive pole. The early ones:

  • Trust vs mistrust (0–1): consistent care builds trust that the world is safe.
  • Autonomy vs shame/doubt (1–3): the toddler asserts independence ("me do it"); supported, they gain confidence; over-controlled, they feel shame.
  • Initiative vs guilt (3–6): taking initiative in play and tasks.
Key study / evidence

Broad clinical and cross-cultural observation rather than controlled experiments. The framework is widely used in developmental and counselling practice because it fits lived experience across the lifespan.

Criticisms

Hard to test scientifically and the ages are loose. Descriptive rather than explanatory — it says what happens but less about how or why. Some stages reflect mid-20th-century Western assumptions.

A1Toddlers sit squarely in autonomy vs shame/doubt — insisting on doing things themselves, resisting help, testing limits.
Sprouts · Erikson's 8 Stages of Psychosocial Development

Chomsky — Language development

language · nature (nativist)

The capacity for language is innate — children are born wired to acquire grammar.

LADuniversal grammarpoverty of the stimulus
Core idea

Children acquire language too fast, and from too little and too messy input, to be explained by imitation and reinforcement alone (the poverty of the stimulus). Chomsky proposed an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD) and a universal grammar underlying all languages. This was a direct attack on Skinner's behaviourist account of language as learned habit.

Key study / evidence

Children produce novel sentences they have never heard and make systematic "virtuous errors" (e.g. "goed", "foots") that show they are applying rules, not copying. Language milestones appear in a similar sequence across cultures.

Criticisms

The LAD is a hypothetical construct with no identified biological basis. It ignores social interaction — input matters more than Chomsky allowed (Bruner's "LASS", the Language Acquisition Support System). Usage-based theorists argue general learning can explain grammar without innate universal grammar.

A1Only worth using if the clip shows notable vocalisation or word combination — but it is excellent for the nature-vs-nurture evaluation.
BBC Radio 4 · Noam Chomsky on Language Acquisition

Freud — Psychosexual development

psychodynamic · stages

Early childhood experiences, driven by unconscious drives, shape adult personality.

id / ego / superegooral · anal · phallicfixation
Core idea

Personality forms through five psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital), each focused on a body zone. Unresolved conflict causes fixation that persists into adulthood. The psyche has three parts: the id (drives), ego (reality), and superego (conscience). Relevant to toddlers: the oral (0–1, mouthing) and anal (1–3, control, toilet training) stages.

Key study / evidence

Built from clinical case studies (e.g. Little Hans) rather than experiments — rich detail, but from a small, unrepresentative sample interpreted by Freud himself.

Criticisms

Largely unfalsifiable — it cannot be tested or disproved (Popper's critique; Eysenck, Farrell). Based on biased case studies, over-emphasises sexuality, and has weak empirical support. Best used in your essay as the theory you critically evaluate, not the one you rely on.

A1Mouthing objects (oral) or control/independence around the anal stage can be named — then critiqued as hard to verify.
Khan Academy · Freud's Psychosexual Development

Also worth knowing

optional extras
Bandura — Social learning theory

Children learn by observing and imitating models, not only by direct reinforcement. The Bobo doll studies showed children copied adult aggression. Bridges behaviourism and cognition (attention, retention, reproduction, motivation). Use if the clip shows clear imitation.

Bronfenbrenner — Ecological systems

Development is shaped by nested environmental layers — microsystem (family), mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem (culture), chronosystem (time). Good for framing context, but hard to observe directly in a short clip.

Research ethics

Why ethics — and the cases that shaped it

Part 1, ch 3

Modern research ethics grew out of serious historical abuses. Knowing the landmark cases is how you recognise a breach.

informed consentdeceptionprotection from harmconfidentialityright to withdrawdebriefing
The landmark cases
  • Little Albert (1920): conditioning fear in an infant, with no debriefing or protection from harm.
  • Tuskegee (1932–72): withholding treatment from men with syphilis, without informed consent.
  • Nazi medical experiments → the Nuremberg Code (1947): the origin of informed, voluntary consent.
  • Milgram (1961–63): obedience study — heavy deception and psychological harm.
  • Stanford Prison (1971): harm and a lack of safeguards; stopped early.
  • Scheper-Hughes (1977), Cyril Burt (twin data): anonymity/consent in ethnography, and fabricated data (integrity).
The codes you cite

PSI (Ireland) and BPS (UK) each have four principles — respect for rights & dignity, competence, responsibility, integrity. APA (US) has five — beneficence & nonmaleficence, fidelity & responsibility, integrity, justice, respect for rights & dignity. All share the same core standards.

The core principles to apply

for Task 3
The standards that recur across all three codes
  • Informed & voluntary consent: participants understand the purpose, risks and their right to withdraw, and agree freely — with no coercion or undue inducement (e.g. a reward so large it overrides free choice).
  • Deception: avoid where possible; if used, justify it and debrief afterwards.
  • Protection from harm: physical and psychological.
  • Confidentiality & data use: anonymise; get consent for recording and for any reuse of data (e.g. showing footage later).
  • Right to withdraw at any time without penalty.
Limits of guideline-led ethics

Codes are necessary but not sufficient: they can be treated as a box-ticking exercise, and they can miss the power relations between researcher and participant (a concern raised by feminist research ethics). Ethical practice is judgement, not just compliance.

A1 Task 3For each scenario: name the one main breach, explain why it's problematic, cite the relevant PSI/BPS/APA principle, and propose an alternative that still meets the aim. Things to weigh in the two scenarios: how participants were recruited and rewarded, whether they consented and to what, whether they were observed or recorded without knowing, and how the data were used afterwards. Decide the issues yourself — that judgement is the assessed skill.

Quantitative methods (for Tasks 1 & 2)

Variables & hypotheses

Part 1, ch 4
IVDVlevelsextraneous variablenull vs alternative
Core idea

The independent variable (IV) is what differs between groups (name its levels); the dependent variable (DV) is what you measure. An extraneous variable is anything else that could affect the DV — control it by randomisation, standardising procedures, or counterbalancing. The null hypothesis says there is no difference; the research (alternative) hypothesis predicts one (directional or non-directional).

In both A1 scenarios the IV has two levels and the groups are separate people — a between-groups (independent-samples) design.

Testing a difference between two groups

SPSS
The method
  • Check assumptions first: level of measurement, independence, normality (Shapiro-Wilk), and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test).
  • If met: an independent-samples t-test. If normality is violated: the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test.
  • Report in APA: group means and SDs, the test statistic, degrees of freedom, the p-value, and an effect size.
Presenting it

Put ONE APA-formatted table and ONE APA chart (e.g. a bar chart with error bars) in the main report. Every piece of SPSS output goes in the appendices — missing output fails the task.

A1 Tasks 1 & 2In SPSS: enter a grouping variable + the score, run Explore for the assumption checks, then Independent-Samples T Test (or Mann-Whitney U), and build the figure in Chart Builder. Answer exactly what each sub-question asks, concisely.

SPSS Guide

independent samplesassumptionsAPA output
Why this matters

A1 Tasks 1 & 2 are 70 of the 100 marks, and both are the same design: two separate groups compared on one continuous score (mindfulness vs control; under-30 vs over-40). This is the exact SPSS workflow for both. You have Laerd Premium, which has the full step-by-step with screenshots — linked below. Run the analysis yourself and write your own interpretation (no AI), and remember: all SPSS output goes in the appendices or the task auto-fails.

Step 1 · Set up the data
  1. Create the variables (Variable View)
    Make a grouping variable (e.g. "Condition", Measure = Nominal) and add Value labels (e.g. 1 = Mindfulness, 2 = Control). Make a score variable (e.g. "RT_ms", Measure = Scale).
  2. Enter the data (Data View)
    One row per participant: their group code and their score. Each person appears once (independent groups).
Step 2 · Check the assumptions
The independent t-test's assumptionsContinuous DV · two independent groups · independence of observations · no significant outliers · normality of the DV in each group · homogeneity of variance. The first three come from the study design; you test the last three in SPSS.
  1. Run Explore for outliers & normality
    Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Explore. Put the score in Dependent List and the grouping variable in Factor List. Under Plots, tick Normality plots with tests and Histogram, and set Boxplots to Factor levels together. Continue → OK.
  2. Read it
    Shapiro-Wilk (use it when each group n < 50): if Sig. is above .05 for both groups, normality is met. The boxplots show outliers (points beyond the whiskers).
  3. Decide which test
    Assumptions met → independent-samples t-test (Step 3a). Normality clearly violated, or problematic outliers → Mann-Whitney U (Step 3b). Homogeneity of variance is checked by Levene's test, which appears inside the t-test output itself.
Step 3a · Independent-samples t-test
  1. Run the test
    Analyze → Compare Means → Independent-Samples T Test. Score → Test Variable(s); grouping variable → Grouping Variable → Define Groups (1 and 2). OK.
  2. Pick the right row using Levene's test
    If Levene's Sig. is above .05, read the "Equal variances assumed" row. If it is .05 or below, read the "Equal variances not assumed" row.
  3. Report from that row
    t, df, Sig. (2-tailed) = your p-value, the mean difference and its 95% CI. SPSS (v27+) also reports Cohen's d for effect size.
Step 3b · Mann-Whitney U (if normality is violated)
  1. Run the test
    Analyze → Nonparametric Tests → Legacy Dialogs → 2 Independent Samples. Score → Test Variable List; grouping variable → Grouping Variable (Define Groups 1, 2); Test Type = Mann-Whitney U. OK.
  2. Report
    U, z and the Asymp. Sig. (p-value), and quote medians (from Explore) rather than means. Effect size: r = z / √N.
Step 4 · The APA chart & table
  1. One chart, in the main report
    Graphs → Chart Builder → Bar → Simple. Drag the grouping variable to the X-axis and the score to the Y-axis. In Element Properties set Statistic = Mean and tick Display error bars (Confidence intervals, 95%). OK, then tidy the axis titles to APA style.
  2. One table, in the main report
    Do not paste raw SPSS output — build a clean APA table (group Ns, means, SDs). The full SPSS output belongs in the appendices.
APA write-up — the shape of the sentenceReport it in this form, filled with your own numbers: "A [independent-samples t-test / Mann-Whitney U test] compared [score] between the [group 1] and [group 2]. [State whether the assumptions were met.] There was [a / no] statistically significant difference, t(df) = __, p = __, d = __." Per-task checklist

Run this for Task 1, then again for Task 2 — the steps are identical. Each tick maps to a marked sub-question.

  • Data entered — grouping variable (with value labels) + score variable, one row per participant.
  • (a) Null and alternative hypotheses written (and stated as directional or non-directional).
  • (b) IV named with its two levels, and the DV stated.
  • (c) One extraneous variable identified, with a specific way to control it.
  • (d) Assumptions tested and reported in full — normality (Shapiro-Wilk), outliers (boxplot), homogeneity of variance (Levene's).
  • Correct test chosen — independent t-test if assumptions hold, Mann-Whitney U if normality is violated.
  • (e) ONE APA table and ONE APA chart (bar chart with error bars) in the main report.
  • (f) Main analysis run and reported in full — t, df, p, Cohen's d (or U, z, p, r).
  • All SPSS output pasted into the appendices (missing output = automatic fail).
  • APA formatting and referencing checked; answers concise and directly on the question.
